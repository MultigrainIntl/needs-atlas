FAQ & what this tool does

Needs Atlas turns official public data into a defensible picture of health and economic need across Zufall Health's seven-county service area — and points to the funding to act on it.

Everything it does, at a glance

  • Interactive need map — all 682 census tracts across the seven counties, shaded by a composite Need Score, with the selected county outlined. Click any tract for its numbers.
  • County dashboard — population, poverty, uninsured, under-200%-poverty, SNAP, limited-English, and age-65+ rates, plus a 0–100 county Need Index and a bar chart ranking all seven counties.
  • Food insecurity — county and child food-insecurity rates from Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap.
  • Community health burden — adult prevalence of 17 chronic-disease, mental-health, and social-need measures from the CDC (PLACES), each compared to New Jersey.
  • Statewide standing — where each county ranks among all 21 New Jersey counties on those health measures.
  • Highest-need tracts — the neighborhood-level table that drops straight into a grant narrative.
  • Downloadable assessments — a formatted Word needs-assessment for each county, fully cited, generated from the same data.
  • Funding matches — open federal grant opportunities from Grants.gov, ranked by fit to each county's needs, with a plain "why it fits" case and closing dates.
  • What's new — a running log of changes as the tool evolves.
  • Secure accounts — sign-in with administrator-approved access for new users.

Where do the numbers come from?

The U.S. Census Bureau (American Community Survey and QuickFacts), Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap, and the CDC's PLACES project — all official, public sources. Funding opportunities come from the federal Grants.gov catalog. Nothing is hand-entered or invented, and the About page lists every source with direct links.

How current is it?

An automated pipeline re-pulls the data and rebuilds the site on a fixed quarterly schedule; the funding opportunities refresh more often. The "Data & updates" panel always shows the date of the most recent refresh.

Can I trust the numbers?

Yes — and you can check them yourself. Every figure traces to a specific public table you can open (the About page shows exactly how to verify each one), and the dashboard and the downloadable documents read from the identical data, so they never disagree.

What are "Funding matches"?

For the selected county, the tool pulls open federal grant opportunities and ranks them by how well they fit that county's documented needs — pairing each with the specific local statistics that make the case. It surfaces opportunities worth reviewing; it does not determine eligibility, so always confirm details on Grants.gov before applying.

Who is it for?

Built by Food Aid Project, Inc. in partnership with Zufall Health, for the grant writers and program staff who need defensible numbers and a shortlist of fundable opportunities in minutes rather than days.

What it doesn't do

The figures are survey- and model-based estimates, best read as strong signals rather than exact counts, and the Need Score ranks need within these counties. It's a decision-support tool — not a substitute for professional grant writing or clinical judgment.

About Needs Atlas

Needs Atlas turns official U.S. Census data into a plain, comparable picture of health and economic need across Zufall Health's seven-county service area — so a grant writer can find the hardest-hit neighborhoods and cite defensible numbers in minutes.

Needs Atlas is built by Food Aid Project, Inc., whose mission is Food and Economic Security through Decision Support. This tool is that mission in practice: it takes public data that is technically open but hard to actually use, and turns it into decisions that grant-makers, health centers, and food-security programs can act on.

What you're looking at

Every figure on this site describes real places: the seven New Jersey counties Zufall serves (Essex, Hunterdon, Middlesex, Morris, Somerset, Sussex, and Warren) and the 682 census tracts inside them. A "census tract" is a small, neighborhood-sized area of a few thousand people that the Census Bureau uses for reporting. For each tract and county we show the share of residents who are uninsured, living on a low income, over 65, or in a household with limited English — the conditions that drive demand for a community health center.

Where the data comes from

Nearly every number on the dashboard is pulled directly from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) — the government's official, ongoing survey of American households (the one exception, food insecurity, is described just below). We use the 2019–2023 five-year estimates (the most reliable version for small areas), pulled straight from the Census Bureau's public data API. Nothing is hand-entered, invented by us, or bought from a third party. The map's tract boundaries come from the Census Bureau's own TIGER/Line cartographic files. The county headline figures — the numbers in the tiles up top and in the downloadable assessments — use the Census Bureau's published county values (QuickFacts), so the dashboard and the documents always show the same number; the neighborhood-level work (the map, the tract need scores, the concentration tables) is the live census-tract pipeline.

The data is not distributed as PDFs — it lives in interactive tables and an open API, so we link straight to the source. These are the exact Census tables behind each indicator; each link opens the live table on the Census Bureau's own site:

  • Uninsured rate — Table B27001 · Health Insurance Coverage by Sex by Age
  • Low income (under 200% of the poverty line) — Table C17002 · Ratio of Income to Poverty Level
  • Below the poverty line — Table B17001 · Poverty Status
  • Limited-English households — Table C16002 · Household Language
  • Age 65 and over — Table B01001 · Sex by Age
  • Median household income — Table B19013
  • Total population — Table B01003
  • SNAP / food-stamp receipt — Table B22003 · Receipt of Food Stamps/SNAP

Browse everything at data.census.gov, see the full list of variables we pull in the ACS API definitions, or find the map boundaries under the Census Bureau's Cartographic Boundary Files.

Food insecurity — Feeding America

One headline figure comes from a single non-Census source: Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap, the standard county estimate of how many residents — and how many children — live in food-insecure households. We use their latest release (2024 estimates). Because Map the Meal Gap is modeled at the county level, food insecurity is shown as a county figure (there is no neighborhood breakdown), and you can look up any county yourself on their interactive map.

Chronic disease & health burden — CDC PLACES

The "community health burden" section draws on the CDC's PLACES project — the standard source of small-area health estimates. For each county we show the adult prevalence of chronic conditions (diabetes, obesity, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, coronary heart disease, stroke, asthma, COPD, cancer, arthritis, and depression), overall health status (fair-or-poor health, frequent mental distress), and health-related social needs (uninsured adults, lack of transportation, food insecurity, and housing insecurity), each compared to New Jersey. The NJ figure is a population-weighted average across all 21 counties. We use the latest county release (2025) and show crude prevalence — the actual share of local adults affected.

An important honesty note: PLACES values are model-based estimates, not a direct count. The CDC builds them by combining its national health survey (BRFSS) with local demographics, which is what makes neighborhood-level health data possible at all — but they are best read as reliable indicators of relative burden rather than exact local tallies. These health measures are shown alongside the need score for context; the county Need Index itself stays a transparent blend of poverty, uninsured rate, and food insecurity.

Finding funding — matched opportunities

The "Funding matches" view turns the need data into a funding to-do list. It pulls open federal grant opportunities from the U.S. government's official Grants.gov catalog and ranks them for the selected county by how well they fit that county's documented needs — pairing each opportunity with the exact local statistics that make the case. A county with elevated food insecurity surfaces nutrition and food-assistance grants first; one with high behavioral-health burden surfaces mental-health and substance-use programs. Each match shows the closing date and a plain "why it fits" line drawn from the county's Census, Feeding America, and CDC PLACES numbers. It surfaces opportunities worth reviewing — it does not determine eligibility, so always confirm the details on Grants.gov before applying.

Statewide standing

Because health need is relative, the community-health section also shows where a county ranks among all 21 New Jersey counties (using the CDC PLACES measures). That lets an application state plainly that a county is, say, among the highest-need in the state on food insecurity or housing instability — a stronger, more defensible framing than a comparison against only its neighbors.

Sources we're still adding

A few more indicators would round out the picture. They're being wired in next, and until each one is connected we list it as pending rather than show a guessed number — the same principle that governs the whole tool:

  • Households that work but can't afford the basics — United Way, United For ALICE
  • Primary-care, dental & mental-health provider-to-population ratios — County Health Rankings & Roadmaps
  • Local chronic-disease and maternal/infant detail beyond the CDC PLACES county estimates — NJ State Health Assessment Data (NJSHAD)

How to check a number yourself

You never have to take our word for it. To verify, say, the uninsured rate we show for Essex County:

  1. Open data.census.gov.
  2. Search the table code — for the uninsured rate, B27001 — and choose the 2023 ACS 5-Year Estimates.
  3. Add the geography you care about: a whole county (e.g. "Essex County, New Jersey") or a single tract.
  4. The uninsured count is the sum of the "No health insurance coverage" rows; divide it by the total to get the percentage. Our pipeline does exactly that sum, automatically, for every tract.

For a CDC PLACES health figure, open data.cdc.gov, search "PLACES County", open the latest release, and filter to your county and the measure you want — the crude-prevalence value is the number we show, and we compare it to the New Jersey average.

How the Need Score is calculated

The tract Need Score (0–100) is a simple, transparent blend of six census-tract need indicators: the uninsured rate, the share under 200% of the poverty line, the poverty rate, the SNAP/food-stamp participation rate, the share age 65+, and the share of limited-English households. For each indicator we place every tract on a 0-to-1 scale, where the tract with the highest value in the seven counties scores 1 and the lowest scores 0 (this is called min–max normalization). We then average the six and multiply by 100. A tract scoring 80 doesn't mean "80% of anything" — it means that, blending these six needs, it sits near the top of the service area.

The county Need Index in the bar chart is built the same way but from three county headline indicators — the poverty rate, the uninsured rate, and the Feeding America food-insecurity rate — compared across the seven counties. That's why the highest-need county scores 100 and the lowest scores 0: the index is relative, ranking counties against each other rather than against an absolute standard. (Food insecurity is a county estimate, so it shapes the county ranking but not the individual tract scores.)

How it stays current

An automated pipeline re-pulls the Census data and rebuilds every number and the map on a fixed schedule — the first of January, April, July, and October each year. (The ACS survey itself only updates once a year, so a quarterly check is more than enough to stay current.) The "Data & updates" panel always shows the date of the most recent successful refresh and when the next one is due. No manual work is required for the numbers to stay fresh.

Who can see this tool

Needs Atlas sits behind a sign-in. The underlying data is public — it's official Census and Feeding America information anyone can look up — so the sign-in isn't about secrecy; it's governance: it keeps a record of who is using the tool and presents it as the professional, curated resource it is. New users can request an account from the sign-in screen; each request is reviewed and approved by the administrator before access is granted, and a bot check on the request form keeps automated sign-ups out.

Checks & balances

  • One official source. Every figure traces to a specific Census Bureau ACS table you can open yourself — no blended or proprietary inputs.
  • Fully reproducible. The pull-and-calculate code is open and runs automatically; the same inputs always produce the same numbers, and the site and the downloadable Word assessments read from the identical data.
  • Transparent math. The Need Score is a plain average of normalized rates — no hidden weighting and no black-box model.
  • Noise filtered, not hidden. The "highest-need tracts" tables skip tiny or non-residential tracts (dorms, prisons, and the like, which produce misleading rates), while the map still shows every tract.
  • Nothing invented. Where the Census has no reliable value for a small tract, the site shows a dash rather than a guess.

Honest limitations

ACS figures are survey estimates, not a full count, so small tracts carry real margins of error — treat a single tract's number as a signal, not a precise measurement, and lean on county figures for the most stable comparisons. The Need Score ranks need within these seven counties, so even a "low" score here still describes a community with real needs. And because the five-year window (2019–2023) is the newest reliable ACS vintage, it can lag very recent local changes. The CDC PLACES health figures are model-based small-area estimates rather than direct counts, so they too are best treated as strong signals of relative burden, not exact local measurements.

Needs Atlas is one of those decision-support tools, developed in partnership with Zufall Health to strengthen its grant work across northern and central New Jersey.
